Where public projects show up before the RFP

A public project usually leaves a paper trail months before procurement. Here are the documents to watch, and what each one tells you.

October 20, 20264 min read

By the time a public RFP is posted, most of the important decisions have already been made. The budget is approved, the scope is mostly settled, and the owner often has a clear idea of who they'd like to work with. Firms that hear about the project on posting day spend the next few weeks catching up.

The good news is that public projects rarely appear out of nowhere. They pass through a series of public documents first, and each one is a chance to find out early.

Capital budgets and long-range plans

Most municipalities, school boards and public agencies publish a capital budget, and many publish a multi-year capital plan alongside it. If a new fire station, school or treatment plant upgrade is coming, it usually appears here first as a line item with a rough cost and a target year. It's the earliest public sign that money is being set aside.

Council and board agendas

Before money gets spent, elected officials usually vote on it. Council and board agendas, and the staff reports attached to them, often include the project's background, its budget, the planned procurement approach and a rough timeline. Staff reports are especially useful because they explain why the project exists, which is exactly what a strong proposal needs to address.

Funding announcements

When a higher level of government funds a local project, there's usually a public announcement. A provincial or state funding commitment for a new school, for example, tells you the project has moved from wish list to real.

Master plans and asset management plans

Hospitals, campuses, transit agencies and water utilities publish plans describing what they'll build or replace over the next five to twenty years. These are slower signals, but they show where an owner's priorities are heading, which helps you decide where to build relationships.

Permits, rezonings and development applications

On the private side, the earliest public trace of a project is often a rezoning or development application. These are filed before the full consultant team is in place, which makes them one of the few public signals for private work.

Why this is hard to do by hand

None of these documents are secret. The problem is volume. A single region can have dozens of councils, boards and agencies, each publishing long PDFs on its own schedule and its own website. Most BD teams don't have the hours to read them all, so they rely on relationships and posted RFPs.
