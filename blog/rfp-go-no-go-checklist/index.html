The go/no-go decision: a simple RFP scoring checklist for AEC firms

Ten questions to score any RFP in a few minutes, so your team spends its time on the pursuits it can actually win.

October 22, 20263 min read

Every RFP your team responds to costs real time and money, and most firms don’t win most of what they chase. Unanet’s 2025 AEC Inspire Report found firms win about half the bids they pursue, and only 40% use a formal go/no-go process.

A go/no-go checklist won’t make a weak pursuit strong. What it does is stop your team from spending a week on a proposal it was never going to win, so that time goes to the ones it can.

The checklist

Score each question 0, 1 or 2. Be honest. The point is to catch the pursuits you want to win but probably won’t.

Does it fit where we want to grow?Not reallyPartlySquarely
Can we write a strong response in time?Very rushedTight but doableYes
Do the evaluation criteria suit us?Mostly lowest priceBalancedWeighted to qualifications

Reading the score

  • 15 to 20: go. Put your best people on it.
  • 10 to 14: discuss. Decide what would have to be true for you to win. If you can’t fix the weak spots before the deadline, pass.
  • Under 10: pass, politely. If it’s a client you want, send a short note saying you’re interested in future work. That costs very little and keeps the door open.

Why the first two questions matter most

If your scores keep coming in low on “do we know the client” and “did we know about it early”, that tells you something. The problem is timing: you’re finding out about projects at the same moment as everyone else.

The fix happens months before the RFP: tracking projects as they move through budgets and council approvals, and getting to know the owner while they’re still deciding what they want. Read how AEC firms win work before the RFP is posted for how to do that.

How to make it stick

  • Decide fast. Aim to make the call within two working days of the RFP landing. A late “go” leaves less time to write.
  • Have one person own the decision. Usually a principal or BD lead, with input from whoever would lead the project.
  • Write the score down. Keep a simple log of every RFP, its score, whether you went and whether you won. After six months you’ll see which questions actually predict your wins, and you can adjust the checklist to match.

The best go/no-go decision is one you make before the RFP exists. When you’ve been following a project for months, you already know most of these answers.
