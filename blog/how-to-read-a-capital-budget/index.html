How to read a capital budget to find upcoming AEC projects

A capital budget shows years of upcoming public projects in one document. Here’s what to look for on each line, and how to turn it into a pursuit list.

October 22, 20264 min read

Most public projects show up in a capital budget long before anyone writes an RFP for them. If you know how to read one, you can see a city’s or school board’s next few years of work laid out in a single document, along with a rough idea of when each project will need consultants and contractors.

Capital budgets look intimidating, but you only need to find a handful of things in them. Here’s how.

Capital budget versus operating budget

Public bodies usually publish two budgets. The operating budget pays for day-to-day costs like salaries, utilities and maintenance. The capital budget pays for building or replacing things that last: new facilities, major renovations, roads, water infrastructure and large equipment.

For finding new projects, the capital budget is the one you want. Many municipalities and school boards also publish a multi-year capital plan alongside it, often covering five to ten years. That longer plan is where you’ll find projects that are still a few years out.

Where to find it

Start on the owner’s own website. Look for pages called budget, finance, capital plan or long-term financial plan. If you can’t find it there, search the council or board agendas from budget season. The full budget is usually attached to the staff report that council votes on.

Budgets are long. Search the PDF for the kind of work you do, like “fire station”, “library”, “school”, “treatment plant” or “renovation”, rather than reading every page.

What to look for on each line

Every project line tells a small story. These are the parts worth writing down.

  • The project name and location. Often short and vague, like “Station 6 replacement”. Make a note anyway. Later documents will fill in the details.
  • The total estimated cost. This tells you whether the project is the right size for your firm. Early estimates often change, so treat it as a range, not a number.
  • The cash flow by year. This is the most useful part. Many budgets show how much will be spent in each year. A small amount in the first year followed by large amounts later usually means design comes first and construction follows. That first small amount is often the consultant fee.
  • The funding source. Reserves, debt, development charges or grants from another level of government. If a project depends on a grant that hasn’t been confirmed, it’s less certain.
  • Words that describe the stage. Look for terms like feasibility, needs assessment, design, tender or construction. They tell you where the project is right now.

Reading the timing

Put the cash flow and the stage together and you can make a reasonable guess about when the buying happens. If a project shows feasibility money this year and design money next year, the owner will likely need a design consultant within the next year or so. If design money was spent last year and construction money starts next year, the design team is probably already in place, and the next buyer is the contractor.

This isn’t exact. Councils defer projects, costs rise and priorities change. But it gives your team a rough calendar of what’s coming, which is far better than waiting for the RFP.

Turn it into a list your team can use

A simple spreadsheet is enough to start. Use one row per project, with these columns:

  • Owner, project name and location
  • Estimated cost and the years money is planned
  • Current stage, and the page of the budget where you found it
  • Your best guess at when consultants or contractors will be hired
  • Who at your firm should follow it

Keep the page reference. When you talk to the owner later, being able to point to the exact line shows you’ve done your homework.

What to do once you’ve found a project

A budget line is a starting point. From there, look for the staff report that introduced the project, which usually explains why it exists and who in the organization is responsible. Watch the council or board agendas for updates. And if the owner holds public information sessions or industry days, go.

The aim is to understand the project and become a familiar name to the people running it, well before procurement starts. That’s much harder to do once an RFP is out and the owner’s communication rules kick in.
