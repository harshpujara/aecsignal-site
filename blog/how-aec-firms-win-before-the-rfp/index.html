How AEC firms win work before the RFP is posted

Many owners decide who they’d like to work with before proposals are submitted. Here’s what strong BD teams do in the months before an RFP.

October 22, 20263 min read

Ask any experienced BD lead how they win their biggest projects and you’ll hear some version of the same answer: they knew the client, and they knew the project was coming long before the RFP.

That’s not just anecdote. Shipley’s Capture Guide, one of the standard references in proposal management, puts it plainly: “40 to 80 percent of the time, customers decide whom they would prefer to buy from before proposals are submitted.” And research from QorusDocs found AEC firms attribute 50 to 74 percent of their revenue to existing clients.

So the real question is how to use the months before the RFP well, and within the rules.

The window that matters

For most public projects there’s a gap of months, sometimes years, between the money being approved and the RFP being issued. During that time, the owner is working out what they need: the scope, the budget, the schedule and how they’ll buy it. They talk to people. They look at what other owners have built. That’s your window.

What strong BD teams do in that window

1. Know what’s coming

You can’t prepare for a project you haven’t heard of. Strong teams track the owners they care about through capital budgets, council and board agendas, funding announcements and planning applications. Our guide on reading a capital budget shows where to start.

2. Learn the project from the public record

Before you talk to anyone, read the staff reports. They usually explain why the project exists, what problems it needs to solve and who is responsible for it. Walking into a conversation already knowing this changes how the owner sees you.

3. Ask for a conversation, not a sale

Owners are wary of firms that show up only to pitch. Offer something useful instead: a lesson from a similar project you’ve done, a question you’d want answered early, or a short case study of something that went wrong elsewhere and how it was fixed.

4. Show up where the owner is

Public information sessions, industry days, association events and open houses are all chances to meet the people running the project in a setting where it’s normal to talk.

5. Write a short capture plan

For each project you’re serious about, keep a one-page plan: what the owner cares about, who the decision makers are, who you think you’re up against, which of your projects and people fit best, and what you’ll do in the next 30 days.

6. Start the proposal early

Once you know the project, you know most of what the proposal will need: relevant past work, the right team, and the themes the owner cares about. Gathering that now means the RFP becomes a matter of tailoring, not starting from scratch.

Know the rules

Early conversations are normal and expected, but public procurement has rules. Once an RFP is issued, most owners only allow questions through the named contact. Some municipalities also have lobbying rules, such as Toronto’s lobbyist registry. Check what applies to each owner, and keep all contact transparent and professional.

When to walk away

Some projects won’t be a fit, however early you hear about them. If your pre-RFP work tells you the owner already has a strong favourite, or the scope isn’t right for you, that’s useful too. It saves you from a pursuit you wouldn’t have won. Our go/no-go checklist helps make that call.

The firms that win consistently tend to have one thing in common: they started months earlier.
