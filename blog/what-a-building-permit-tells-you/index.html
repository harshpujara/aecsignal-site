What a building permit tells you about the next RFP

One building permit can mean months of work for contractors, trades and furniture dealers. Here’s who it matters to, and when.

October 22, 20263 min read

When a building permit is issued for a new office, school or bank branch, it looks like the end of a long process. For a lot of firms, it’s the start of one. A single permit can mean months of work for contractors, trades, furniture dealers and specialists who haven’t been hired yet.

Here’s how to read a permit as an early sign of work coming, and which firms it matters to most.

What a permit actually tells you

A building permit means the design is largely finished. To apply, the owner needs a full set of drawings, so the architect and engineers have usually been on the project for a long time already. If you’re an architecture or engineering firm, a permit is mostly a record of work someone else won.

But a permit also means construction is close, and a lot of the buying happens from here on.

Who still needs to be hired

Depending on the project and how the owner buys, a permit can be the starting gun for:

  • General contractors or construction managers, if one isn’t already on board
  • Trades and subcontractors, from mechanical and electrical to finishes
  • Commercial furniture dealers, since furniture is usually bought late, close to when people move in
  • AV, IT, security and access control specialists
  • Signage and wayfinding
  • Commissioning agents, movers and relocation planners

For these firms, a permit gives you something most of your competitors won’t have: time. You know the project is real, roughly how big it is, and what kind of building it will be, months before the owner starts asking for quotes.

What to look for in permit data

Permit records vary by city, but most include some of the following:

  • The address and type of building, such as office, institutional or retail
  • The estimated construction value
  • A description of the work: new building, addition or interior alteration
  • The owner or applicant, and sometimes the designer or contractor
  • The date issued

Many cities, including Toronto, publish permit data on their open data portals, so you can search it without paying for a lead service.

An example

Say a permit is issued for a new two-storey bank branch. The construction value tells you the size. The description tells you it’s a new building, not a renovation. If the permit names the designer, you know which architecture firm is shaping the interior.

For a furniture dealer, that’s a lead with a clear path: get to know the designer, who often influences what’s specified, and the bank’s facilities or real estate team, who will do the buying. And you’re doing it while the building is still going up, months before the quote request arrives.

If you’re an architect or engineer

Permits come too late for design work, so look earlier in the life of a project. Capital budgets, rezoning applications, site plan applications and council approvals all come before design is complete. Our guide on reading a capital budget covers where to start for public projects.

The right signal at the right time

Every firm on a project gets hired at a different point. A rezoning matters to an architect, a site plan approval to a contractor, and a permit to a furniture dealer. The trick is knowing which signal matters to you, and watching for it.
